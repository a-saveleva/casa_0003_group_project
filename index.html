Developing a web tool to inspire sustainable journeys into British nature by rail networks.

Our project aims to contribute to the ongoing discussion on reducing car dependence and promoting sustainable transportation options, particularly by increasing the use of public transport for leisure trips to natural spaces outside cities. We demonstrate the natural assets that are easily accessible by train to help people make more sustainable travel choices without compromising on the quality of their leisure. Our tool features an interactive map displaying natural assets within the user's chosen travel time, allowing exploration of destinations and their suitability for different types of outdoor activities.

↓

Problem overview

Despite growing interest in sustainable lifestyles, access to natural spaces in the UK remains largely car-dependent — especially for longer trips.

The People and Nature Survey found 66% used active travel (walking, cycling, mobility aids) on their most recent visit, but nearly half of those trips were to green spaces within one mile. For visits over 10 miles, 87% relied on passive transport, a category that includes both private vehicles and public transit, making it difficult to assess sustainable access.

More targeted data reveals the scale of car dependence: the National Parks for All report found 93% of National Park visitors arrived by car. This has environmental and equity implications, as road transport is the UK’s top source of emissions, and one in four households lacks a car.

Among non-visitors, 24% cited transport barriers — 14% didn’t own a car, 10% found destinations too distant or inaccessible. This highlights how, without a car, the dominance of car-dependent destinations often results in non-visitation.

Understanding the user needs

In order to provide the most tailored experience using this tool and ensure the users are truly willing to take the train, we have studied what people are looking for when selecting a nature destination.

Urgency

In recent years, there has been a noticeable willingness among the public to adopt more sustainable travel behaviors, particularly in response to climate change. According to a 2023 Department for Transport report , between one-third and one-half of respondents expressed openness to:

  • walking more (50%)
  • using public transport more (38%)
  • driving less (36%)
  • limiting flying (31%)

However, the same report notes a decline in this willingness compared to the previous year, suggesting that without sustained support and improved infrastructure, the initial momentum for behavioral change may be waning. For instance, enthusiasm for driving less was lower in November 2023 than in November 2022.

This highlights the urgency of action: if the current barriers to sustainable travel are not addressed, the UK risks losing public goodwill and sliding back into more car-dependent patterns of leisure travel.

Our response

We have developed an online tool that displays natural assets within the user's chosen travel time, allowing exploration of destinations and their suitability for different types of outdoor activities. The interactive map is backed by a selection of datasets and research on user needs.

The map uses TravelTime API to calculate accessible areas, with inclusion of a short walk on start and end of the journey. The travel time can be adjusted with a slider. After the accessible area is generated, the natural assets within it are highlighted. The user can hover and click on the asset to learn more about it.

A key element of the project is classifying natural assets. Drawing on surveys such as the People and Nature Survey and national polls, we reviewed over 40 common outdoor activities. From these, we selected seven key activities that are both popular and supported by geospatial data, allowing us to assign scores to each natural asset. We excluded activities that lacked consistent national datasets (e.g. stargazing, berry picking). Each natural asset is assigned a score based on suitability for each of those activities. The scores can be viewed by hovering on the asset.

Methodology

Isochrone building and Personalized Route Visualization

Isochrones are generated based on rail travel time using the TravelTime API. Users can interactively select any point on the map and specify a travel duration (up to 4 hours), upon which an isochrone is calculated accordingly. Based on the isochrone, users can explore green assets of interest; when hovering over a green asset, the system identifies the nearest departure station to the user and the nearest arrival station to the target green asset using railway and station data sourced from OpenStreetMap. Additionally, a railway network graph is constructed, and the shortest travel route is computed using Dijkstra’s algorithm in NetworkX. This enables personalized route visualization for train-based green asset access.

Dataset of Natural Assets

To address the lack of open data on publicly accessible natural areas, we used OpenStreetMap to extract natural features across Great Britain using:

  • Boundary: protected_area
  • Leisure: park, nature_reserve, garden
  • Natural: beach, forest, wood, wetland, scrub, hill, valley, heath, grassland

This was supplemented with official SSSI datasets. We merged fragmented features into larger assets, filtered out marine or inaccessible areas, and excluded unnamed ones. Only assets over 100 hectares were retained.

Scoring the Natural Assets

We used thematic indicators to score assets across multiple categories:

  • Hiking - Long-distance walking routes (Walking Englishman)
  • Cycling - Cycle paths and National Cycle Network (OSM)
  • Birdwatching - Sightings, IBAs, and RSPB reserves
  • Camping - Camping POIs within 2 km
  • Seaside - Bathing waters and beach awards
  • Geology - Geodiversity and Geological Society’s top sites
  • Best Overall - Composite of all scores

All indicators (except geology) include an ecological value score, normalized to a 0–1 range, and averaged.

Limitations and discussion

Discussion

This project set out to explore how digital tools could promote sustainable leisure travel in the UK by making nature more visible and accessible via the existing rail network. Through a mix of geospatial analysis, data integration, and user-focused design, we created an interactive map that highlights nature destinations reachable within a rail-based travel time, supported by contextual information about each site’s recreational and ecological value.

Key contributions include:

  • Bespoke map combining UK natural assets with rail travel times;
  • A novel aggregation method to enhance OpenStreetMap’s fragmented representations of natural features.
  • A category-based scoring system to help users choose destinations aligned with their interests;
  • A modular, responsive web application that prioritises usability, visual appeal, and data transparency.

Each team member brought different expertise to this interdisciplinary tool, blending transport data science, ecological insight, design, and user engagement. The resulting tool is not only technically functional but also accessible, visually compelling, and grounded in the user experience of planning low-impact trips.

Limitations of the Data Sources

The interactive map is fully functional, but several limitations warrant careful consideration of findings. The main concern lies in the quality and completeness of the natural assets dataset, which relies heavily on OpenStreetMap contributions and is thus susceptible to input errors. Since some indicators are also sourced from OpenStreetMap, they inherit similar limitations.

To reduce reliance on precise boundaries, future versions could adopt a more abstract data format, such as grids or point-based representations. Indicator quality could also be enhanced by incorporating more curated datasets. If OpenStreetMap remains a core source, improvements could come from more advanced queries and higher-resolution data.

While shape aggregation improved usability and readability, it also resulted in the loss of detailed attributes (e.g., names, Wikipedia links). Asset naming remains a challenge, but automated approaches based on dominant features and location could help assign meaningful names.

Network Representation

While isochrones are generated using the TravelTime API based on rail travel time, the underlying station and track data used by the API are not publicly disclosed. In contrast, the network graph and route visualizations in this project are independently constructed using railway and station data extracted from OpenStreetMap. As a result, there may be discrepancies between the isochrone coverage and the network visualization. Additionally, some minor stations or track segments may be missing from OSM, or their positional inaccuracies may lead to mismatches in the inferred rail connectivity. These limitations introduce a degree of uncertainty into the route calculations and network representations.

Limitations Arising from the Tool’s Scope

Like many targeted tools, our solution is not fully independent from traditional trip planning methods. The isochrone feature, for instance, only supports rail and limited walking, excluding key modes such as buses. These omissions are due to time and scope constraints. However, for future or commercial applications, the tool would need to support all major transport modes and enable multimodal interchanges.
